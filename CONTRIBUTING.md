# Contributing to DisabilityWiki

The most useful thing you can add here is the practical, local knowledge you already have.

Federal law is well covered on this wiki, and it is the easiest part to write, because it is
already written down everywhere. What is thin is everything specific and practical: how a
particular state's waiver process actually works, which paratransit systems will and will not
cross a county line, what a 504 meeting looks like when the district is stonewalling, which
pharmacy in a given county still fills a specific prescription.

That knowledge mostly lives in people's heads and in group chats. Putting it here is the point.

## Who owns what you write

You do. Contributors retain copyright in their own contributions.

Content is licensed **CC BY-SA 4.0** (see [LICENSE](LICENSE)). In plain terms:

- You keep attribution for what you wrote.
- Anyone can reuse and adapt it, as long as they credit you and license their version the same way.
- It **cannot be taken private, paywalled, or made proprietary later** — not by a future maintainer,
  and not by the repository owner. CC BY-SA is irrevocable and derivative works must carry the same
  terms. If that ever mattered, the content could be forked and hosted by someone else entirely.

If you would rather contribute without your name attached, say so and it will be credited as
anonymous or to an organization instead.

**Write it in your own words.** The one thing that cannot be accepted is text copied from
somewhere else — an agency's page, a nonprofit's guide, another wiki. Not because it is not
useful, but because we cannot license what we do not own, and one pasted paragraph puts the
whole page's reuse terms in doubt. Link the source instead and say what it means in your words;
that is more useful to the reader anyway. Facts you read somewhere — a phone number, a deadline,
a dollar figure — are free to state, and citing where you got them helps the next person.

## How to contribute

**You do not need to learn a wiki editor.** Pick whichever is easiest:

1. **Just talk.** Describe what you know in an email, a voice memo, or a conversation, and it will
   be written up as a page for your review before anything is published.
2. **Send a document.** Any format. It will be converted.
3. **Open a pull request.** Content lives as markdown in this repository if you prefer git.
4. **Open an issue.** Describe what is wrong or missing on a page and someone will write it up.

There are no accounts and no login — the site is static by design, so it stays fast and
works offline. Nothing about you is collected; the only thing the site records is an
anonymous per-page count from the "Was this page helpful?" question, with no IP, cookie,
identifier or timestamp ([detail](https://disabilitywiki.org/privacy)). In-browser suggested edits are
[planned](docs/contribution-model.md) but not built yet; until then the routes above are how
contributions arrive.

## What makes a good page

- **Specific beats general.** "Call this office, ask for this form, expect this wait" is worth more
  than a summary of the statute.
- **Say when you learned it.** Processes change. A date lets the next person judge staleness.
- **Link the primary source** where one exists: the statute, the agency page, the form itself.
- **Name the friction.** The part where the process fails people is usually the part nobody
  documents, and it is the most useful thing on the page.

## Crisis and life-safety pages

Pages under `crisis/` hold hotline numbers people reach for in an emergency, and they are held to
a different standard: **every number must be verified against the operator's own current page
before it lands**, with the date recorded. A wrong number here is worse than a missing one. If you
find a number that has changed or been disconnected, that is one of the most valuable
contributions you can make — say where you checked and when.
